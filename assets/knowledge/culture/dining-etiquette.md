# Dining etiquette

Shared meals, chopsticks, toasts, and paying the bill.

## Shared dishes

- Meals are usually shared: dishes sit in the middle and everyone takes from them.
- A lazy Susan may be used; turn it slowly and avoid turning it while someone is serving.
- Ordering more food than the group can finish signals generosity from a host.
- Soup is drunk from the bowl with a spoon, not lifted and drunk straight.

## Chopsticks

- Do not stand chopsticks upright in rice; it resembles incense at a funeral.
- Do not tap the bowl with chopsticks or point them at people.
- Rest chopsticks on the rest or across the bowl edge; do not leave them crossed.
- Use the serving spoon when one is provided; otherwise take from the shared dish cleanly.

## Seating and starting

- The host or an elder often signals the start of the meal and takes the first bite.
- A guest of honour may be seated facing the door or at the head of the table.
- Elders are served first.

## Toasting

| Pinyin | English | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `gan1bei1` | cheers / bottoms up | formal toast |
| `sui2yi4` | as you like | lets others sip instead |
| `qing3 man4 yong4` | please enjoy | host to guests |
| `man4man4 chi1` | eat slowly / enjoy | warm parting line |

- A toast may be offered to the table or to one person; the glass is often held lower than
  an elder's as a sign of respect.
- `sui2yi4` is the polite release from a full `gan1bei1`.

## Tea and the bill

- Tea is refilled for others before oneself; a light tap of two fingers on the table
  thanks the pourer.
- The bill is often contested, with one person paying for all; splitting is now common
  among younger diners.
- Tipping is not expected.

## Authoring notes

- Dining scenarios work well for the LAMP loop: rehearse ordering, toasting, and paying as
  a single short exchange.
- Keep `gan1bei1` for festive contexts and `qing3 man4 yong4` for host politeness.
- Do not present these as absolute rules; families and regions differ.
