# Greetings and etiquette

Everyday meeting, addressing, and politeness conventions.

## Greetings

- `ni3 hao3` works at any time of day; time-specific greetings (`zao3shang5 hao3`,
  `wan3shang5 hao3`) are warmer.
- A common casual greeting is `chi1 le5 ma5` (have you eaten?), a friendly check-in rather
  than a real question about food.
- Handshakes are common in formal and business settings; a slight nod or a small bow of
  the head is enough in casual ones.

## Addressing people

- Use a surname plus a title or term: `Wang2 xian1sheng5` (Mr Wang), `Li3 lao3shi1`
  (Teacher Li).
- Given names alone are for friends, family, and peers; a first meeting stays with titles.
- Kinship terms extend to strangers as friendly address: `a1yi2` for an older woman,
  `shu1shu5` for an older man.

## Everyday politeness

| Pinyin | English | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `qing3` | please | before a request |
| `qing3 wen4` | may I ask | opener with a stranger |
| `ma2fan5 ni3` | sorry to trouble you | request or thanks |
| `bu4 hao3yi4si5` | excuse me / my apologies | mild apology, getting attention |
| `xie4xie5` | thank you | |
| `bu2 ke4qi5` | you're welcome | |

## Visiting and public behavior

- Bring a small gift when invited to someone's home (see `gift-giving.md`).
- Remove shoes at the door when the household does; follow the host.
- Queues are observed in cities; on escalators, stand to one side and walk on the other.
- Tipping is not expected in restaurants or taxis.
- Topics such as politics and Taiwan are best avoided in casual conversation; follow the
  other person's lead.

## Authoring notes

- Keep greetings short and warm; the app's field missions can open with `ni3 hao3` or a
  time greeting.
- Pair `bu4 hao3yi4si5` with interruption and request scenarios, not with serious apology.
- Regional and generational variation is significant; describe the custom rather than
  prescribing it.
