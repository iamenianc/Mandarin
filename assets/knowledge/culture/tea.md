# Tea

Tea is the default drink of hospitality. Offering and refilling tea is a small ritual that
recurs in homes, offices, and restaurants.

## Types

| Pinyin | English |
| --- | --- |
| `cha2` | tea |
| `lü4cha2` | green tea |
| `hong2cha2` | black tea (literally red tea) |
| `wu1long2` | oolong |
| `pu3er3` | pu'er, a fermented tea |
| `mo4li4hua1cha2` | jasmine tea |
| `hua1cha2` | scented tea |

## Hospitality

- A guest is offered tea on arrival; accepting is the easy social choice, and declining is
  done with thanks and a reason.
- The host pours for guests first and refills cups before they empty.
- A light tap of two fingers on the table thanks the person pouring; it is a quiet gesture,
  not a spoken phrase.
- At a restaurant, tea is often brought automatically and refilled without asking.

## Tea talk

| Pinyin | English |
| --- | --- |
| `he1 cha2` | to drink tea, or to meet over tea |
| `cha2yi4` | the art of tea |
| `yi1 bei1 cha2` | a cup of tea |
| `zai4 lai2 yi1 bei1` | one more cup, please |

`he1 cha2` often means socializing rather than drinking; an invitation to have tea is an
invitation to talk.

## Authoring notes

- Keep tea phrases with the greeting and dining themes; they recur in every hospitality
  scenario.
- `hong2cha2` is literally "red tea"; note this when translating black tea for the learner.
- A tea offer is a good first listening drill: short, predictable, and polite.
