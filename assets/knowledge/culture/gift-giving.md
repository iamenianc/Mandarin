# Gift giving

What to bring, how to present it, and what to avoid.

## Occasions

- Bring something small when invited to someone's home: fruit, tea, sweets, or a modest
  gift for the household.
- Red envelopes (`hong2bao1`) with money are standard for Lunar New Year, weddings, and
  birthdays, given by elders to younger people.
- On a visit, a gift for a child of the household is always welcome.

## Presentation

- Offer and receive gifts with both hands.
- The recipient may politely decline once or twice before accepting; this is courtesy, not
  refusal.
- Traditionally a gift is not opened in front of the giver, though younger people often
  do. Follow the recipient's lead.
- Gifts are often wrapped in red or gold for celebrations.

## What to avoid

| Avoid | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Clocks | `song4 zhong1` (to give a clock) sounds like attending a funeral |
| White or black wrapping | funeral colors |
| Sharp objects, such as knives | suggest cutting a relationship |
| Shoes | said to send the recipient away |
| Umbrellas | `san3` (umbrella) sounds like `san4` (to separate) |
| Quantities of four | `si4` sounds like `si3` (death), see `numbers-and-taboos.md` |

Lucky quantities: even numbers, and eight in particular. For cash gifts, avoid amounts
containing four.

## Authoring notes

- Keep gift scenarios to a visit or a festival; the vocabulary is small and concrete.
- When Raymond answers a gift question, name the taboo and the reason together, so the
  learner understands the wordplay.
- Preferences vary by region and generation; present these as common customs.
